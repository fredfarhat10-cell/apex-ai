# Evidence Review: Does LLM/Agentic Trading Work, Do Multi-Agent Architectures Help, and Do "Learning" Mechanisms Improve Decisions? (as of 25 Oct 2026)

Method note for the report writer: research was done 25 Oct 2026. Direct page fetches were blocked for most domains in this environment (arXiv, nof1.ai, alphaarena.ai, forklog, substack all returned DNS/403 errors), so most findings come from search-engine renderings of abstracts, official leaderboards and secondary coverage. Where only secondary sources were available this is flagged. The one primary page fetched in full was the open-source Alpha Arena replication on GitHub. Evidence labels used throughout: **Verified** (primary source / official record), **Strong evidence** (multiple peer-reviewed or consistent independent studies), **Anecdotal** (single short-window event, blogs, vendor claims), **Speculative** (plausible but untested), **Unknown**.

## Q1. Live and benchmark results: what happened, and what were the methodological weaknesses?

### Takeaway
No live, public, statistically meaningful evidence shows that general-purpose LLMs (alone or as agents) produce persistent risk-adjusted alpha after costs. The live contests (Alpha Arena S1/S1.5) ran 2–3 weeks with 6–8 models, which is far too short and too few to separate skill from luck. The more careful benchmarks (StockBench, LiveTradeBench, InvestorBench) mostly find LLM agents fail to beat buy-and-hold, and they find that general LLM ability does not predict trading ability. Overall label: **Strong evidence that "it doesn't reliably work yet"; Unknown whether it can.**

### Cited Findings

**Critiques of Alpha Arena** (**Strong evidence** that the conclusions are not statistically supportable)
- Open-source replication (Eug-Chua): Claude Sonnet 4.5 and GPT-4o-mini were each run at two temperatures, with 10 trials per configuration, on Oct 17–30 2025 data. Claude at temperature 0.7 returned between −35.9% and +55.6% (SD 35.5%) **on the same data with the same prompt**. Only 1 of 6 pairwise comparisons was significant at p<0.05. The backtest assumes no slippage and may contain look-ahead. The author estimates production-grade evidence needs 3–6 months, 30–50 trials per configuration and multiple regimes — [GitHub: llm_trading_arena](https://github.com/Eug-Chua/llm_trading_arena) (fetched in full)
- Critiques note a sample of 6 models over a very short horizon, with no tools or outside information given (S1), and that luck can dominate for years — [Boris Tseitlin, Substack](https://borisagain.substack.com/p/why-alpha-arena-is-literally-the); [Medium critique](https://krishnakumar-sharma.medium.com/when-ai-models-got-10k-each-and-permission-to-yolo-trade-3c38d65d4133)
- The dashboard excluded unrealised P&L until positions closed, and Sharpe readings were low or negative — [search summary of Buildix/iweaver coverage](https://www.buildix.trade/blog/nof1-alpha-arena-lessons-ai-trading-byok-strategy-advisor-2026)
- Commentators note that the short duration, leverage, regime, prompting and small model count make it "a benchmark result, not an investable performance record" — [iweaver S1 results](https://www.iweaver.ai/blog/alpha-arena-ai-trading-season-1-results/)

**StockBench (Chen, Yao et al., arXiv 2510.02209, Oct 2025)** (**Strong evidence**, peer-review status unknown)
- Agents made daily buy/sell/hold decisions on DJIA stocks over multiple months, seeing prices, fundamentals and news. Models included GPT-5, Claude-4, Qwen3, Kimi-K2 and GLM-4.5. **Most models failed to beat buy-and-hold.** Strong scores on static financial QA did not translate into trading performance. The test data is Mar–Jul 2025, chosen to avoid training-data overlap — [arXiv 2510.02209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02209v1); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v1)
- Limitations: a small fixed universe of large caps, a short window, and no high-frequency or multi-cycle coverage — same source. I could not retrieve per-model return numbers (see Gaps).

**LiveTradeBench (Yu, Li, You; arXiv 2511.03628, Nov 2025)** (**Strong evidence**)
- 50-day **live** evaluation of 21 LLMs on US stocks and Polymarket. LMArena scores were uncorrelated or negatively correlated with trading outcomes (Spearman ρ ≈ 0.05 for stocks, −0.38 for Polymarket). Sharpe correlation between the two markets was about 0. GPT-4.1 had the best stock return but underperformed on Polymarket — [Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2511.03628); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/397322519_LiveTradeBench_Seeking_Real-World_Alpha_with_Large_Language_Models)
- A related live benchmark ("When Agents Trade", arXiv 2510.11695) found that agent frameworks show distinct risk behaviours, while the model backbone contributes less to outcome variation — [arXiv 2510.11695](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11695)

**InvestorBench (arXiv 2412.18174) and FinBen (arXiv 2402.12659)** (**Strong evidence** of weakness, with a contamination caveat)
- InvestorBench tested 13 LLMs on stocks, crypto and ETFs. Proprietary and larger models did better, but **all struggled in volatile markets**. Results are the median of 5 runs — [arXiv 2412.18174](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.18174). StockBench criticises this line of work for using pre-2021 data, which raises contamination risk — [StockBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v1). On a third-party aggregation, buy-and-hold (34.1%) ranked 6th of 14 entries — [benchmarklist (InvestorBench)](https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/investorbench/) (secondary; unverified)
- FinBen (15 LLMs initially): GPT-4 led on stock trading, but all models struggled with complex extraction and forecasting — [arXiv 2402.12659](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2402.12659v2)

**"LLMs as traders": the canonical academic signal paper** (**Strong evidence** of a decaying signal)
- Lopez-Lira & Tang, "Can ChatGPT Forecast Stock Price Movements?" A gross return of 400%+ (Oct 2021 – Dec 2022) was reported **without transaction costs**. The v6 update (Oct 2025) reports strategy returns **declining as LLM adoption rises**, which is consistent with arbitrage. Press coverage cites about 50% outperformance at 25 bp costs (figures vary by version) — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6); [UCLA draft](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/document/2024-04/4.19.24%20Alejandro%20Lopez%20Lira%20ChatGPT_V3.pdf); [i3 Invest](https://i3-invest.com/?p=10796)

**Meta-evidence from surveys (2026)** (**Strong evidence** that the literature is weak)
- "Agentic Trading: When LLM Agents Meet Financial Markets" (arXiv 2605.19337). The v1 audit covered 77 studies screened through 9 Mar 2026: **only 2 of 19** empirical studies had extractable time-consistent split protocols, **only 1 of 19** reported an explicit transaction-cost model, and **no study achieved the highest (R3) reproducibility tier**. The v2 version expanded to 185 studies, with 80 closed-loop systems — [arXiv 2605.19337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19337); [Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/agentic-trading-when-llm-agents-meet-financial-markets) (the v1 statistics come via a secondary summary)
- "Agentic Quantitative Trading" survey (arXiv 2608.31041): strong model or forecasting capability "does not reliably translate into trading performance under live market conditions" — [arXiv 2608.31041](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.31041v1)

**Live-money AI funds** (**Anecdotal**, and not LLM-based)
- AIEQ (an AI-powered ETF on IBM Watson, launched Oct 2017; a quant model, not an LLM): 64% total return since inception vs 75% for the S&P 500 TR by 2021. A 2025 analysis found "no consistent alpha" and holdings not meaningfully different from SPY — [WealthManagement/Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/etfs/ai-fund-that-was-trouncing-market-is-derailed-by-tech-bets); [Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4797167-aieq-the-ai-etf-that-thinks-but-doesnt-outperform-yet)

### Inferences
- Alpha Arena is best read as a **marketing and behaviour-observation experiment**, not as evidence of skill. With 43 trades for the S1 winner, the dispersion seen in the replication (a ±45 percentage-point range from identical inputs) dominates any between-model difference.
- The most robust cross-study finding is a **negative** one: general LLM leaderboard ability does not predict trading outcomes (LiveTradeBench, StockBench, Agentic Quant survey). A product should not market "smarter model = better trades".
- The agent framework (risk rules, sizing, leverage, cash discipline) appears to explain more outcome variance than the model choice ("When Agents Trade"). This points to risk architecture as the defensible lever, not model selection.
- The only published "LLM alpha" with academic weight (news sentiment) appears to be decaying as adoption grows. That is classic alpha decay.

### Gaps
- Exact per-model return and Sharpe tables for StockBench and LiveTradeBench could not be retrieved, because arXiv full text was blocked.
- Alpha Arena Season 2 (2026) results could not be verified. nof1.ai and alphaarena.ai were unreachable. No Nof1 technical post-mortem with statistics was found.
- No audited, live-money, LLM-driven fund track record of at least 1 year was found.

## Q2. Pitfalls (look-ahead/memorisation, overfitting, survivorship, costs, cherry-picking) and the evidence standard a skeptical quant would accept

### Takeaway
LLM backtests on any period before the model's training cutoff are **fundamentally non-identifiable**: the model may simply remember outcomes, and masking or "pretend it's 2020" prompting does not fix this (**Strong evidence**). Documented leakage wipes out roughly half or more of reported agent performance. A credible standard is live or strictly post-cutoff out-of-sample testing, pre-registered, with realistic costs, enough independent bets, benchmarks, and multiple-testing-adjusted statistics (Deflated Sharpe, t>3).

### Cited Findings
- **Memorisation is provably confounding.** Lopez-Lira, Tang & Zhu, "The Memorization Problem" (Apr 2025 – Apr 2026 versions): LLMs recall exact pre-cutoff values of macro indicators, and memorisation extends to headlines, stock returns and conference calls. Instructions to respect date boundaries fail. **Masking fails, because LLMs reconstruct entities and dates from minimal context.** No recall is observed after the cutoff. The authors prove non-identification: once realised values were in training, forecasting skill cannot be recovered from outputs — [arXiv 2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2); [SSRN 5217505](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=5217505); [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/the-memorization-problem-can-we-trust-llms-forecasts/) (**Strong evidence**)
- **Leakage inflates agent results.** "Profit Mirage" (Li et al., arXiv 2510.07920, Oct 2025): back-tested returns of LLM financial agents fade once the knowledge window ends. FinMem total-return decay was up to 71.85%. A secondary summary reports Sharpe decay of 51–62% for leading agents. FinMem and FinAgent gave highly consistent predictions under counterfactual input perturbation, which indicates memorisation. The paper releases FinLake-Bench — [arXiv 2510.07920](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07920); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07920v1); [Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/profit-mirage-revisiting-information-leakage-in-llm-based-financial-agents) (**Strong evidence**; the Sharpe figure is secondary)
- Benhenda (2026), Look-Ahead-Bench: LLMs recall in-cutoff S&P 500 prices nearly verbatim. Purpose-built point-in-time models delivered more stable alpha across periods — [arXiv 2601.13770](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.13770); cited in [FinCAD, arXiv 2605.24564](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24564)
- **Mitigations (none proven sufficient):** DatedGPT trains 1.3B models with strict annual cutoffs from 2013–2024 — [arXiv 2603.11838](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.11838v1). FinCAD uses inference-time decoding to suppress "parametric look-ahead" — [arXiv 2605.24564](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.24564). Merchant & Levy use logit adjustment via small "forget/retain" models — [arXiv 2512.06607](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06607v1) (**Speculative** as complete fixes)
- **The "distraction effect" can matter more than look-ahead.** Glasserman & Lin: anonymised headlines **outperformed** named headlines in-sample, especially for large firms. The model's prior knowledge of a company biases its reading of the news — [arXiv 2309.17322](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322) (**Strong evidence**). Note that Lopez-Lira et al. later show masking does not fully remove memorisation (above).
- **Benchmarks of the full evaluation-failure list.** Nguyen & Pham (arXiv 2603.27539, Mar 2026) list look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, backtest overfitting, transaction-cost neglect and regime-shift blindness, and argue these "can reverse the sign of reported returns". They propose a "Coordination Breakeven Spread" for whether multi-agent value survives costs (analytical only; no new empirics) — [arXiv 2603.27539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27539)
- Agents trained on historical news without ingestion-lag modelling or embargo enforcement are likely to show inflated backtests — [arXiv 2605.19337](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19337v1)
- **Multiple testing and the hurdle rate.** Harvey, Liu & Zhu (RFS 2016): new factors need t > 3.0, not 2.0, and "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false" — [NBER w20592](https://nber.org/papers/w20592); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2513152) (**Strong evidence**)
- **Deflated Sharpe Ratio** (Bailey & López de Prado, JPM 2014) corrects for selection bias from multiple trials and for non-normal returns — [SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551). The **Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio and Minimum Track Record Length** give the track length needed to reject SR ≤ threshold at a given confidence. Negative skew and fat tails lengthen it, and for typical hedge-fund distributions it can exceed several years of monthly data — [Journal of Risk](https://www.risk.net/journal-risk/2223785/sharpe-ratio-efficient-frontier); [Portfolio Optimizer blog](https://portfoliooptimizer.io/blog/the-probabilistic-sharpe-ratio-bias-adjustment-confidence-intervals-hypothesis-testing-and-minimum-track-record-length/) (**Verified**)
- An LLM strategy-design benchmark finds real backtests are worse than LLM-claimed ones (blog) — [Jiri Pik, Jul 2026](https://jiripik.com/2026/07/11/llm-designing-trading-strategies/); see also [AlphaForgeBench, arXiv 2602.18481](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18481) and [CLQT cost-aware benchmark, arXiv 2606.29771](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29771) (**Anecdotal/Strong**, details not retrieved)
- **Costs ignored at the source:** the TradingAgents paper's own footnote says each prediction used 11 LLM calls and 20+ tool calls, so the backtest was limited to about 3 months. Its Sharpe "exceeds our expected empirical range", which the authors attribute to few pullbacks — [arXiv 2412.20138](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20138)

### Inferences (proposed evidence standard a skeptical quant would accept)
1. **Strictly post-cutoff data**: the test period starts after the latest training cutoff of every model used, including embedding models. Better still, run live forward tests with timestamped decision logs (hash-committed before the outcome).
2. **Pre-registration**: the strategy, universe, prompt and model versions, sizing rules, metrics and stopping rule are fixed and published before the test. Every variant tried is counted for the DSR.
3. **Enough independent bets**: inference from Lo-style Sharpe standard errors (SE(SR) ≈ sqrt((1+SR²/2)/T) with T in years for an annualised SR). An annual SR of 1.0 needs about 4 years for t≈2 and about 9 years for t≈3. An SR of 2 needs about 1.5–3 years. For a hit-rate claim, distinguishing 55% from 50% at α=0.05 with 80% power needs about 780 independent trades; 52% vs 50% needs about 4,900. This is my own calculation from standard power formulas and should be checked by the writer. Alpha Arena's 43 trades are therefore uninformative.
4. **Costs**: fees, spread, slippage or market impact, funding rates (perps), borrow, plus **LLM inference cost per decision** (relevant to multi-agent systems).
5. **Benchmarks**: buy-and-hold, equal weight, a simple momentum or trend rule, and a single-LLM baseline at equal compute.
6. **Statistics**: Deflated Sharpe / PSR, t>3 per Harvey-Liu-Zhu for new claims, multiple seeds (LLM sampling variance is large, as the replication shows), reporting of max drawdown and tail metrics, and multiple regimes.
7. **Leakage tests**: counterfactual perturbation (Profit Mirage style) and point-in-time data, with survivorship-free universes.
- Under this standard, **no LLM trading result published as of Oct 2026 that I found qualifies**. Confidence: high that none of the prominent ones do; medium that none exist anywhere.

### Gaps
- Benhenda Look-Ahead-Bench quantitative results were not retrieved.
- No pre-registered LLM trading study was found.
- Exact per-period Sharpe-decay numbers in Profit Mirage come from secondary reviews.

## Q3. Multi-agent vs single-agent: does adding agents help, and when does it stop helping?

### Takeaway
Multi-agent gains are **task-dependent, small at equal compute, and often negative for sequential reasoning**. Early "debate improves factuality" results did not control for compute. Controlled studies find equal-budget self-consistency or single agents match or beat debate in many settings, while homogeneous agents show conformity and sycophantic collapse. In trading, multi-agent claims (TradingAgents etc.) rest on short, cost-free backtests without rigorous ablations. Label: **Strong evidence** that "more agents" is not reliably better. **Unknown** for an optimal agent count in trading. No study tests 1 vs 3 vs 7 vs 12 agents in live trading.

### Cited Findings
- **Original pro-debate result.** Du et al. (ICML 2024, arXiv 2305.14325): multiple model instances critique and revise over several rounds, which improved math, strategic reasoning and factual validity and reduced hallucinations — [arXiv 2305.14325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14325) (**Verified** result, but no equal-compute baseline)
- Critique: Du et al. never present an equal-budget baseline. 2025 rebuttals reportedly showed equal-budget single agents match debate — [Beancount research log, May 2026](https://beancount.io/bean-labs/research-logs/2026/05/24/multiagent-debate-factuality-reasoning-llms) (secondary)
- **Equal-compute negative result.** Huang et al., "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet" (ICLR 2024): with an equal number of responses, multi-agent debate **significantly underperforms** self-consistency with majority voting. Debate is better understood as a way to reach "consistency" — [arXiv 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01798) (**Strong evidence**)
- **Equal-compute positive result (2026).** "Multi-Agent Reasoning Improves Compute Efficiency" (ACL 2026 SRW): at equal budget, debate beats self-consistency by **+1.3 points** and mixture-of-agents by **+2.7 points**. Self-consistency saturates earlier, and multi-agent gains persist on harder tasks — [arXiv 2605.01566](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01566); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-srw.1/) (the gains are small)
- **Homogeneous debate fails.** "The Cost of Consensus" (2026; 7–8B models): isolated self-correction matches or beats unguided homogeneous debate at substantially lower token cost, and the consensus step can cost accuracy — [arXiv 2605.00914](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00914v1); [CAIS 2026](https://caisconf.org/program/2026/papers/decomposing-sycophancy-fragility-consensus-collapse-and-cost-in-homogeneous-mult/)
- **Conformity and sycophancy.** Inter-agent sycophancy collapses debates into premature consensus — [arXiv 2509.23055](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23055v1). Across 2,500+ simulations, neutral agents follow the majority, and a single strong model sways them more than a group of weaker ones — [arXiv 2506.01332](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01332v1). BenchForm conformity benchmark — [ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/1da9ca7e9cef4b1af63913f05d1630a4-Paper-Conference.pdf). Sycophancy propagation between agents — [arXiv 2604.02668 (SIGDIAL 2026)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.02668) (**Strong evidence**)
- **Scaling agent systems (Google/MIT, arXiv 2512.08296, Dec 2025).** Over 180–260 configurations, **all multi-agent variants degraded sequential-reasoning performance by 39–70%**. Centralised coordination improved parallelisable tasks, *including financial reasoning*, by **80.9%**. The study shows capability saturation (diminishing or negative returns once the single-agent baseline is strong) and a tool-coordination trade-off (tool-heavy tasks are hit hardest by overhead). Its framework predicts the best architecture for 87% of held-out configurations — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/towards-a-science-of-scaling-agent-systems-when-and-why-agent-systems-work/); [arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.08296) (**Strong evidence**; the finance task is a reasoning benchmark, not live trading)
- **Voting ensembles scale, but plateau.** "More Agents Is All You Need" (TMLR 2024): sampling plus majority vote improves with agent count (Llama2-13B ensemble 59% vs Llama2-70B 54% on GSM8K). Gains rise and then fall with task difficulty, which suggests a complexity threshold. The method is essentially CoT self-consistency — [arXiv 2402.05120](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.05120); [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/more-agents-is-all-you-need-researchers-improve-llms-through-ensemble-of-agents/); [gonzoml](https://gonzoml.substack.com/p/more-agents-is-all-you-need)
- **Why multi-agent systems fail (MAST, NeurIPS 2025).** 1,600+ annotated traces across 7 frameworks show 14 failure modes in 3 categories (specification/system design, inter-agent misalignment, task verification), with κ=0.88. Failures stem mostly from system design, not model limits — [arXiv 2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v2); [NeurIPS](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/121528)
- **TradingAgents (arXiv 2412.20138).** About 3-month backtest; 11 LLM calls and 20+ tool calls per prediction; anomalously high Sharpe that the authors attribute to few pullbacks — [arXiv 2412.20138](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20138). Commentary says there is no ablation isolating debate, roles or communication, no comparison with other agent systems, and no live evidence. A 2026 survey uses its ablations for a "Coordination Primacy" claim and cites its Sharpe as "regime-shift blindness". The sources conflict on whether meaningful ablations exist — [arXiv 2603.27539](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.27539); [search summary](https://tradingagents-ai.github.io/). A crypto paper notes that collaborative designs don't stress-test assumptions adversarially and that single-period Sharpe has large sampling variance — [MDPI Electronics, Consensus-Gated Execution](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/15/3453)
- In live multi-market testing, agent framework explains more behaviour than model backbone — [arXiv 2510.11695](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11695)

### Inferences
- **Practical agent-count guidance** (inference, **Speculative** for trading): 1 strong agent plus deterministic risk rules is the baseline. 3–5 *heterogeneous* perspectives (different models or data views) with independent first drafts and a single aggregator is where the evidence of benefit concentrates (self-consistency and voting saturate within a handful of samples; MoA/debate gains of 1–3 points). At 7–12 homogeneous agents, conformity, token cost (linear or worse), latency and coordination failures dominate. No study shows a benefit at that scale for decision quality in trading.
- Independence matters more than headcount. Homogeneous agents built on the same model share errors. Diversity (different models, data or prompts) plus independent initial answers before discussion is the design principle with evidence behind it.
- Trading decisions are largely **sequential** (state → position → next state), which is the task class where Google/MIT found 39–70% degradation from multi-agent setups. Parallel decomposition (e.g. independent research on separate tickers or news streams) fits the 80.9% gain class.
- Any multi-agent trading claim should be benchmarked against **a single agent at equal token budget** and **net of inference cost** (Coordination Breakeven Spread concept).

### Gaps
- No study found that sweeps the number of agents (1/3/7/12) in a trading context with costs.
- The TradingAgents ablation details could not be confirmed from the primary text.

## Q4. Adversarial/critic agents, devil's advocate prompting, red-teaming before decisions

### Takeaway
Human decision science gives **moderate** support for structured pre-decision dissent: pre-mortems generate more failure reasons and reduce overconfidence, and checklists help in high-reliability fields. It also shows that *role-played* devil's advocacy is weaker than authentic dissent and can entrench the original view. LLM self-critique without external feedback is unreliable. Critic agents add value mainly when anchored to external checks (rules, data, tests). Label: **Strong evidence** that structure helps humans modestly. **Anecdotal/Speculative** for LLM critic agents in trading.

### Cited Findings
- **Pre-mortem / prospective hindsight.** Mitchell, Russo & Pennington (1989) is widely cited as showing that imagining an outcome has already happened raises identification of reasons by about 30%. The measure was the **quantity** of reasons, not their correctness — [Jason Collins, "The premortem"](https://corporate.jasoncollins.blog/premortem). Klein popularised the method (HBR 2007) — [HBR Guide excerpt](https://www.safaribooksonline.com/library/view/hbr-guide-to/9781422187319/14-chap5.xhtml). Veinott et al. (2010; 178 students) found the pre-mortem reduced overconfidence more than comparison methods. The academic literature is thin — [Jason Collins](https://corporate.jasoncollins.blog/premortem) (**Moderate/Anecdotal**)
- **Devil's advocate vs authentic dissent.** Nemeth, Brown & Rogers (2001, EJSP): devil's advocacy tended to produce "cognitive bolstering" of the initial view. Authentic dissent stimulated more original thoughts, more consideration of the opposite view, and more attitude change — [Zendy/EJSP 10.1002/ejsp.58](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/ejsp.58); [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/stop-playing-devils-advocate-and-other-advice-for-better-decision-making/). Most of the evidence comes from Nemeth's lab (**Moderate**)
- **LLM self-critique without external feedback** does not reliably improve reasoning and can degrade it — [Huang et al., arXiv 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01798) (**Strong evidence**)
- **Debate for safety/error reduction.** RedDebate reports that self-critique often plateaus or becomes unstable while debate gives more reliable error reduction. This compares against self-critique, not voting — [arXiv 2506.11083](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.11083)
- A trading-specific argument: collaborative multi-agent designs (TradingAgents) lack adversarial stress-testing, which motivates "consensus-gated execution" in crypto (the MDPI paper's own claims; results not verified here) — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/15/3453)
- Sycophancy makes LLM "critics" prone to folding into consensus — [arXiv 2509.23055](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23055v1)

### Inferences
- An LLM "devil's advocate" is structurally a *role-played* dissenter, which is exactly the variant Nemeth found weakest, and LLM sycophancy compounds this. Critics should be **given different information or objectives** (e.g. a risk agent that can only see drawdown, correlation and liquidity, with a veto bound to hard limits). Using a different model family brings the critic closer to "authentic" dissent.
- The highest-value "critic" is a **deterministic pre-trade checklist / risk gate** (position limits, leverage caps, liquidity, kill-switch). That is what Knight Capital lacked (Q6). An LLM pre-mortem ("assume this trade lost 20%, why?") is cheap and plausibly reduces overconfidence (**Speculative**, no trading RCT).

### Gaps
- No RCT or controlled study found on LLM critic or red-team agents improving trading P&L or decision quality.
- The WHO surgical checklist evidence (Haynes et al., NEJM 2009) was not retrieved in this session. My recollection is that mortality fell from about 1.5% to 0.8%; this is unverified and should not be cited without checking.

## Q5. Learning mechanisms: reflection, memory, journals, calibration, abstention. What genuinely improves, and what is impossible to claim?

### Takeaway
"Learning" via reflection or memory reliably helps only where there is **fast, unambiguous external feedback** (unit tests, game success). Trading feedback is slow, noisy and confounded with luck, and FinMem/FinAgent-style memory gains largely vanish under leakage controls. What *can* improve measurably over time is **process quality**: calibration (Brier scores on explicit, resolvable forecasts), rule adherence, error-rate reduction, and abstention when uncertain. Claims that an agent "learned to trade better" from a few weeks or months of P&L are statistically unsupportable. Label: **Strong evidence** for the limits; **Moderate** for calibration training in humans; **Speculative** for LLM agents.

### Cited Findings
- **Reflexion** (Shinn et al., NeurIPS 2023): HumanEval pass@1 rose from 80% to 91% with GPT-4; AlfWorld 130/134 vs 108/134 for ReAct; LeetCode Hard 7.5% → 15%. The gains depend on ground-truth feedback (reference tests). Self-generated tests create circularity. Weaker models (starchat-beta) showed **no improvement** — [Beancount research log](https://beancount.io/bean-labs/research-logs/2026/04/25/reflexion-language-agents-verbal-reinforcement-learning); [aiwg REF-021](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/aiwg@2026.8.8/docs/references/REF-021-reflexion-verbal-reinforcement.md) (**Verified** numbers via secondary sources; limitations are commentary)
- **FinMem** (layered memory plus reflection) and **FinAgent** (dual-level reflection, multimodal) reported beating DRL and LLM baselines in backtests — [arXiv 2311.13743](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.13743); [arXiv 2402.18485](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2402.18485v2). Under leakage controls, **FinMem total return decayed by up to 71.85%**, and both agents behaved as if memorised (prediction consistency under perturbation). FinAgent was slightly more resilient, attributed to external tools — [Profit Mirage, arXiv 2510.07920](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07920v1) (**Strong evidence** that the memory-agent backtests are inflated)
- **LLM confidence calibration.** Xiong et al. (ICLR 2024): verbalised confidence is **systematically overconfident**. Larger models are better but far from ideal. Consistency-based sampling helps somewhat, nothing wins consistently, and all methods struggle on professional-knowledge tasks. White-box methods are only marginally better (AUROC 0.522–0.605) — [arXiv 2306.13063](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063v2); [ICLR](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2024/poster/18135). Training for calibrated verbal confidence is an active area — [ConfTuner, arXiv 2508.18847](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18847v1) (**Strong evidence**)
- **Human calibration can be trained.** In the Good Judgment Project RCT (Tetlock/Mellers), a roughly 1-hour training module improved accuracy by about 10% (Brier) across four years. This is separate from the team-aggregation gain, also about 10% — [AI Impacts](https://aiimpacts.org/evidence-on-good-forecasting-practices-from-the-good-judgment-project/); [LongTermWiki](https://www.longtermwiki.com/source/303d1e17bc4df5ee) (**Strong evidence**, secondary summary)
- **Selective prediction / conformal abstention.** Conformal abstention gives finite-sample guarantees for LLMs declining to answer — [arXiv 2405.01563](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.01563v1). Learned conformal abstention policies improved hallucination-detection AUROC by up to 22.2% and cut calibration error by over 70% on LLM/VLM benchmarks — [arXiv 2502.06884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06884v1). **No empirical evidence found that abstention improves trading Sharpe.** The one finance-specific preprint reports only labelled *simulated* results as a hypothesis — [chatpaper listing](https://chatpaper.com/paper/314591) (**Unknown** for trading)
- Lopez-Lira et al.: memorisation extends to embeddings, so a retrieval/"memory" store built with a pre-cutoff embedding model can itself leak — [arXiv 2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)
- Run-to-run variance is large. Identical-input Claude runs at temperature 0.7 spanned −36% to +56% over 2 weeks — [GitHub replication](https://github.com/Eug-Chua/llm_trading_arena). Any "after learning, returns improved" claim must exceed this noise floor.

### Inferences
- **Decision quality vs outcome quality ("resulting", Annie Duke's term):** with Alpha Arena-scale samples (tens of trades), P&L cannot validate or invalidate a process. A learning loop that updates on P&L outcomes will mostly learn noise and can actively degrade a sound process after a losing streak. Journals should score **decisions** (was the thesis falsifiable, was sizing within rules, was the forecast calibrated) separately from **outcomes**.
- **What can genuinely be measured as improving** (inference): (a) Brier or log-score of explicit probabilistic forecasts (e.g. "P(price > X at T)") across hundreds of resolvable forecasts, which converges much faster than Sharpe because each forecast is a scored observation; (b) calibration curves and reliability diagrams; (c) process-compliance rates; (d) error taxonomies from journals (repeat-mistake frequency); (e) coverage-vs-error curves for abstention.
- **What is dangerous or impossible to claim:** "the AI learned and now earns X%" from under about 1–3 years of live data; "reflection improved returns" without a no-reflection control at equal compute on post-cutoff data; any backtest-based improvement on pre-cutoff periods (non-identifiable); win rate as proof of skill from under about 500–1,000 independent trades (see the Q2 power calculation).
- **Strategy retirement:** a defensible rule is pre-registered kill criteria (e.g. drawdown beyond a pre-set multiple of expected, or PSR falling below a threshold), not discretionary "it stopped working". The Bailey/López de Prado PSR and MinTRL framework supports this (inference from [Journal of Risk](https://www.risk.net/journal-risk/2223785/sharpe-ratio-efficient-frontier)). Expect alpha decay: the LLM-news signal declined as adoption rose ([Lopez-Lira & Tang v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)).
- Calibration is a credible product feature only if confidence is calibrated empirically (via a sampling-consistency method plus post-hoc recalibration on resolved forecasts). Raw LLM-stated confidence should be treated as overconfident by default.

### Gaps
- No study found measuring Brier-score improvement of an LLM trading agent over time.
- No primary source retrieved for Annie Duke's "resulting". The concept is used here as a framing, not as evidence.
- No evidence found on "trade journal" interventions for retail traders specifically (RCTs).

## Q6. Black swan / catastrophic failure lessons from automated trading

### Takeaway
The catastrophic failures of automated trading came from **missing hard controls, untested code paths and liquidity/leverage feedback loops**, not from bad forecasts. The lesson for LLM agents: deterministic pre-trade risk gates, kill switches, leverage caps and liquidity-aware execution matter more than model intelligence. Label: **Verified**.

### Cited Findings
- **Knight Capital (1 Aug 2012):** in 45 minutes the router sent more than 4 million orders while trying to fill 212 customer orders, traded 397 million shares, acquired billions in unwanted positions and lost more than $460M. The cause was dormant "Power Peg" code (unused since 2003, never deleted) that was re-triggered by a flawed deployment for NYSE's Retail Liquidity Program. 97 automated error emails sent before the open were ignored because they were not designed as alerts. The SEC order (16 Oct 2013) charged Knight under the Market Access Rule (15c3-5) for lacking controls to prevent erroneous orders and enforce capital thresholds, with a $12M penalty — [SEC press release 2013-222](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222); [J.R. Varma](https://www.jrvarma.in/blog/Y2013/Knight-Capital.html)
- **Flash Crash (6 May 2010):** a 75,000-contract E-mini sell program (about $4.1B, identified in media as Waddell & Reed) was executed by an algorithm targeting a share of volume **without regard to price or time**, in about 20 minutes amid thinning liquidity. Liquidity providers withdrew. Intraday Dow declines are reported as about 700–1,000 points — [CS Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/Business/2010/1001/Flash-crash-report-A-tale-of-how-not-to-make-a-big-trade); [CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2010/05/14/markets/flash_crash_waddell_reed/)
- **Crypto liquidation cascade (10–11 Oct 2025):** triggered by the announcement of 100% tariffs on Chinese imports. About $19B in liquidations (official, and called a lower bound); CoinShares says real losses could exceed $50B, with futures open interest falling from $175B to $125B in under a day. BTC top-of-book depth shrank by more than 90% on key venues. Auto-deleveraging mechanisms and high leverage formed a margin spiral. More than 1,000 Hyperliquid wallets were fully liquidated — [CoinShares](https://coinshares.com/insights/knowledge/billions-in-liquidations-what-happened/); [FTI Consulting](https://fticonsulting.com/insights/articles/crypto-crash-october-2025-leverage-met-liquidity); [CoinGecko](https://www.coingecko.com/learn/october-10-crypto-crash-explained); [WisdomTree](https://sitecore10dev.wisdomtree.eu/de-lu/blog/2025-10-17/whats-hot-leverage-liquidity-and-panic); [Proof of Talk](https://proofoftalk.io/blog/crypto-market-fragility-liquidity-leverage/)
- Timing note: Alpha Arena S1 launched on Hyperliquid on 18 Oct 2025, **one week after** this cascade. Its window therefore did not include the largest tail event of the period — [GNCrypto](https://www.gncrypto.news/news/qwen-wins-alpha-arena-season-1-with-22-percent-returns/)

### Inferences
- LLM agents add new failure modes on top of the classic ones: hallucinated tickers or quantities, malformed tool calls, prompt injection via news feeds, API outages or latency during volatility, and correlated behaviour if many users run similar prompts on the same models. That last risk (herding) is consistent with LiveTradeBench's observation that the average cash ratio across all 21 models fell together from 17% to 7.5% during a rally ([Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2511.03628)) (**Speculative** as systemic risk).
- Minimum controls implied by these cases: hard position, notional and leverage limits outside the LLM; order-rate throttles; a kill switch; staged deployment with retesting of all code paths; real alerts rather than emails; liquidity-aware, price-limited execution; and stress tests on Oct-2025-style gaps where stops fill far through their levels.

### Gaps
- No detailed post-mortem found on Hyperliquid's auto-deleveraging behaviour on 10 Oct 2025.
- No documented case found (as of Oct 2026) of a major LLM-agent-caused trading loss incident. Absence of evidence, not evidence of safety.
